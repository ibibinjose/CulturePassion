## I2 — Cold outreach (only with a genuinely specific thesis hook)

**Subject:** `A$58M SAM, 10% plan — cultural participation infrastructure`

> Hi [First name],
>
> I noticed [specific: a portfolio company, a published thesis, a talk they gave] and thought CulturePass might be relevant.
>
> **The problem:** cultural life in multicultural cities runs on WhatsApp groups and printed flyers, so it is invisible to anyone not already inside the community. Organisers can't use mainstream ticketing — the fees consume 15–20% of a A$12 ticket. And councils fund cultural programming then receive back attendance estimates written on the night, because no instrument exists.
>
> **What we built:** discovery, free RSVP and low-fee ticketing, signed-QR passes, and gate check-in that makes attendance verifiable. Then we sell the resulting participation data to the councils and cultural bodies who currently cannot measure it.
>
> **Why it's investable:** A$58M bottom-up Australian SAM, plan captures under 10%. Host CAC A$180 against A$728 three-year LTV — 4:1, built from host-attributable gross profit only, with sponsorship and civic revenue deliberately excluded.
>
> Raising **A$2.5M**. Melbourne pilot [launched September 2026].
>
> Worth 30 minutes?
>
> **Bibin Jose** · Founder & CEO, CulturePass
> [phone] · cultureos.co@gmail.com

**Notes.** The LTV sentence is the highest-signal line available in a cold investor email. Volunteering what you excluded from LTV, unprompted, tells a seed investor more about how you think than three paragraphs of vision.

## I3 — Follow-up with traction (day 14 — the most effective follow-up you have)

**Subject:** `Re: CulturePass — [N] organisations, [N] attendances`

> Hi [First name],
>
> Quick update since I wrote, in case the numbers change things:
>
> - **[N] cultural organisations** onboarded across [N] Melbourne suburbs
> - **[N] events published**, [N] verified attendances
> - **[N]% gate check-in rate** on paid tickets — this is the number that makes the civic product sellable
> - [One specific: a council conversation, a host story, a retention figure]
>
> And one thing that isn't working: [name it honestly — e.g. "paid ticketing adoption is slower than modelled at [N]% against a 26% target; free-event density is where the growth is coming from"].
>
> Still raising A$2.5M. The offer of a ten-minute product walkthrough stands.
>
> **Bibin**

**Notes.** Including something that is not working is counter-intuitive and it is the most effective thing in this email. Investors read hundreds of updates in which everything is going well; the one that names a problem gets read properly and remembered.

## I6 — Responding to a pass

**Subject:** `Re: CulturePass`

> Hi [First name],
>
> Thanks for coming back to me clearly — that's more useful than a slow no.
>
> One question if you have a moment: was the main hesitation [your read of their objection], or something else? I'm trying to work out which objections are about the pitch and which are about the business, and specific feedback is the fastest way to tell them apart.
>
> I'll add you to the monthly update if that's welcome — happy to remove you if not.
>
> Thanks again,
> **Bibin**

**Notes.** Never argue with a pass. Ask one question, request permission for the update list, and move on. The pass is often about stage or portfolio fit rather than the business, and the relationship is worth more than the rebuttal.

**Notes.** With sector angels, the expertise genuinely is worth more than the money — and this framing also happens to be the most effective way to get the meeting. Both things are true, which is what makes it usable.
